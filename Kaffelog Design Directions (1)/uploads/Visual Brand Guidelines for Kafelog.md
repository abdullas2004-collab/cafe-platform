# Visual Brand Guidelines for Kafelog

## Intro

**Brand name:** Kafelog  
**Founder name:** Not shown in the mood board  
**Brand concept:** A profit-protection platform for cafés that turns operational waste, fines, stock loss, inventory leakage, and compliance gaps into visible financial data. The core promise is simple: identify where margin is disappearing, quantify the loss, and give café operators a clear next action.

**Target audience:** Café owners, operators, managers, and hospitality teams responsible for margins, stock control, waste reduction, and daily operational discipline. The brand speaks to people who are commercially minded but still live inside a tactile, fast-moving hospitality environment.

---

## 1. Brand Personality & Emotional Tone

The brand feels **commercial, grounded, sharp, premium, accountable, and quietly urgent**.

Its strongest emotional idea is financial leakage made visible. Milk spills, dripping liquid, paper receipts, handwritten stock notes, and measured quantities turn invisible operational problems into something physical. The result feels tangible rather than abstract.

There is a strong balance between two worlds:

- warm café culture
- disciplined financial control

The hospitality side brings stainless steel, milk jugs, syrup containers, receipts, dark work surfaces, storage shelves, and staff imagery. The analytical side appears through figures, dashboards, alerts, charts, metric cards, reports, and structured interfaces.

The mood is serious without feeling corporate. It avoids bright startup energy. Instead, it feels mature and commercially focused.

Spatially, the system mixes bold editorial scale with dense operational detail. Oversized headlines and financial figures create immediate impact. Smaller labels, reports, interface modules, and handwritten notes add evidence and realism.

Contrast is high. Dark espresso tones sit against warm paper backgrounds. Copper and amber accents behave like both warning signals and signs of value.

The overall feeling is **premium but practical**. It is polished enough to feel trustworthy while retaining enough texture to feel connected to the physical realities of running a café.

---

## 2. Typography Style

Typography is one of the strongest parts of the visual identity.

### Header style

Primary headlines use a **high-contrast editorial serif** with dramatic thick and thin strokes. The letterforms feel refined, confident, and slightly traditional. They borrow visual authority from publishing, finance, and premium hospitality rather than software culture.

Large headline typography is often:

- uppercase
- tightly stacked
- oversized
- high contrast
- used as the dominant visual object

The headline treatment gives short statements weight. Words such as “STOP,” “THE,” and “LEAK” become almost architectural.

Selective color changes inside headlines create emphasis without needing decorative effects. A dark first phrase paired with a warm copper final word creates a useful hierarchy.

### Body style

Body typography is a **clean sans serif**. It is direct, functional, and relatively compact.

This contrast is important:

**Serif = authority, story, financial consequence**  
**Sans serif = explanation, interface, instruction, utility**

Body copy uses short paragraphs and clear line lengths. It feels conversational rather than technical.

### Data typography

Large financial values become their own typographic layer. Numbers such as monthly loss or prevented loss are presented at a dramatically larger scale than surrounding labels.

These values should feel:

- immediate
- undeniable
- measurable
- financially meaningful

Use tabular or highly legible number forms inside dashboards where alignment matters.

### Hierarchy

A recommended hierarchy is:

1. Oversized editorial serif headline
2. Large monetary figure
3. Short sans serif explanatory statement
4. Compact uppercase metric label
5. Small supporting metadata

Avoid excessive font variation. The visual interest comes from scale, weight, contrast, and placement rather than many typefaces.

### Spacing

Headlines can use compressed vertical rhythm for force.

Body copy should receive more breathing room.

Dashboard typography should remain disciplined and compact.

Punctuation should be restrained. Short sentences with periods work well because they reinforce clarity and consequence.

## 3. Color Palette Structure

The palette is built around coffee, milk, paper, metal, and financial warning cues.

### Primary: Deep Espresso Brown

A very dark brown acts as the signature anchor.

**Role:**
- primary typography
- brand marks
- premium dark backgrounds
- strong interface surfaces
- high-contrast framing

**Emotional resonance:** dependable, serious, grounded, hospitality-led.

It feels warmer than pure black, which keeps the identity connected to coffee and physical materials.

### Secondary: Burnished Copper

A textured copper-brown provides the main expressive brand color.

**Role:**
- highlighted words
- large monetary figures
- buttons
- active states
- graphic accents
- data emphasis

**Emotional resonance:** value, cost, heat, coffee, consequence.

The slightly oxidized or printed texture is important. Flat digital orange would feel too generic.

### Dark Background: Charcoal Espresso

The dark UI surfaces are nearly black with a subtle brown undertone.

**Role:**
- dashboards
- mobile interfaces
- promotional cards
- high-impact content blocks

Use warm off-white text rather than stark white.

### Light Background: Warm Paper Cream

The primary light field is a textured cream or parchment tone.

**Role:**
- editorial layouts
- reports
- presentation pages
- long-form content
- data summaries

It should feel like paper, receipt stock, or a lightly weathered financial document.

### Accent: Golden Amber

A brighter amber-orange appears sparingly.

**Role:**
- alerts
- key actions
- selected icons
- CTA buttons
- small chart highlights

It should function as a signal, not a large background color.

### Supporting neutrals

Use:
- stainless-steel grey
- warm taupe
- soft stone
- muted beige
- soot black

These help connect the digital system to café interiors and equipment.

### Palette behavior

The system relies on **high contrast with warm tonal relationships**.

Avoid rainbow data visualization. Keep charts primarily neutral with one warm highlight color.

Gradients should be subtle. Prefer dark brown into charcoal, copper into amber, or cream into warm beige.

## 4. Textural World & Backgrounds

Texture plays a major role in keeping the brand from feeling like generic software.

### Paper textures

Warm paper surfaces should show subtle:

- grain
- receipt marks
- ledger lines
- faint accounting figures
- light distress
- printed imperfections

These backgrounds support the financial story while keeping light layouts tactile.

### Liquid textures

Milk and coffee function as symbolic visual material.

Use:

- suspended pours
- drips
- spills
- puddles
- thin liquid trails
- measured quantities

Liquid imagery should communicate loss, flow, and disappearing margin.

### Industrial café materials

The environmental material world includes:

- brushed stainless steel
- black worktops
- storage containers
- transparent measuring jugs
- stacked stock
- cardboard packaging
- refrigerators
- receipts
- clipboards

These details ground the visual system in daily operations.

### Printed financial texture

Receipts, handwritten counts, invoices, checklists, and report sheets work especially well as layered background elements.

They should feel real enough to imply evidence.

### Surface treatment

Backgrounds should rarely feel digitally perfect.

Use controlled:

- grain
- print texture
- paper fibers
- subtle stains
- light scratches
- warm photographic noise

The texture should support readability rather than compete with it.

## 5. Photographic Direction

Photography is **cinematic, operational, tactile, and documentary-led**.

### Lighting

Use directional naturalistic light with strong shadow structure.

The mood board favors:

- warm highlights
- deep blacks
- reflected stainless-steel light
- controlled contrast
- selective brightness

Scenes should feel intentionally lit without appearing like glossy advertising.

### Human imagery

People should appear working, checking, counting, reviewing, or responding.

Expressions should feel:

- focused
- concerned
- analytical
- attentive

Avoid broad lifestyle smiles or staged team celebrations.

The strongest human image shows a worker caught between service operations and financial review. That tension fits the brand well.

### Object imagery

Objects are equally important as people.

Useful subjects include:

- milk jugs
- measuring cups
- receipts
- inventory containers
- stock shelves
- phones
- reports
- invoices
- clipboards
- spilled liquids

### Composition

Favor:

- tight crops
- macro details
- overhead operational scenes
- close-range environmental portraits
- diagonal visual movement
- layered foreground objects

The images should create the sensation of standing inside the café operation.

### Color treatment

Keep saturation restrained.

Use warm browns, metallic neutrals, cream, black, and amber.

The image grade should feel slightly cinematic and tactile rather than bright, glossy, or heavily filtered.

## 6. Visual Symbols, Icons & Accents

### 1. Droplet motif

The droplet is the clearest recurring symbol.

It can represent:

- waste
- leakage
- cost
- margin loss
- milk
- liquid stock

Use it as a small icon, section marker, data category symbol, or graphic punctuation.

Future versions should remain original rather than copying the existing mark.

### 2. Fine measurement lines

Small ruler marks, tick marks, ledger lines, and measurement scales give layouts a sense of monitoring and precision.

They work especially well on:

- side margins
- data cards
- reports
- infographics

### 3. Financial figures

Large monetary values behave almost like graphic symbols.

Use them as visual anchors rather than simply body content.

### 4. Thin technical icons

Icons are simple, linear, and functional.

Useful themes include:

- timer
- shield
- box
- chart
- alert
- inventory
- document
- measurement

Keep the line weight consistent and restrained.

### 5. Liquid trails

A controlled drip or pour can connect elements vertically across a composition.

This is especially effective in campaigns focused on waste.

Use it selectively so it remains recognizable.

## 7. Mockup Style & Content Applications

The mockups show a system designed to work across both marketing and operational products.

### Website

Desktop layouts use dark interface surfaces with warm cream typography.

The page architecture is modular:

- clear headline
- short value proposition
- primary CTA
- key financial metrics
- alerts
- category summaries
- small trend graphs

The interface feels commercially focused rather than feature-heavy.

### Mobile

Mobile screens prioritize one urgent metric or alert.

A strong mobile structure is:

**Warning state**  
**Financial consequence**  
**Short explanation**  
**Immediate action**

This keeps the mobile experience useful during café operations.

### Reports

Printed or downloadable reports use the lighter paper system.

They should resemble a blend of:

- financial statement
- operational summary
- café receipt

Large savings figures create immediate payoff.

### Social media

Promotional tiles work best with a dark field, editorial serif headline, dramatic liquid image, and one clear CTA.

Avoid trying to communicate the whole product in one asset.

Each piece should focus on a single business consequence.

### Data visualization

Charts are intentionally simple.

Use:

- line graphs
- single highlighted values
- compact metric cards
- ranked cost categories
- savings totals

Decorative analytics should be avoided.

### Overall layout rhythm

The visual rhythm combines **large editorial statements with tightly organized utility modules**.

That contrast is a major strength.

## 8. Overall Brand System Summary

The identity creates a world where café operations and financial visibility occupy the same visual language.

Hospitality supplies the materials:

- milk
- metal
- coffee
- paper
- storage
- staff
- receipts

Financial intelligence supplies the structure:

- metrics
- alerts
- charts
- monetary values
- reports
- categories

Typography connects them.

The editorial serif creates authority and emotional weight. The sans serif creates operational clarity.

Color reinforces the same relationship. Espresso and cream feel familiar to hospitality. Copper and amber turn financial leakage into a visible warning signal.

Texture keeps the digital product connected to real café environments.

Future designers should preserve four core behaviors:

1. Make financial consequences visually immediate.
2. Use physical café materials to give abstract data context.
3. Balance editorial drama with operational clarity.
4. Keep the palette narrow, warm, tactile, and controlled.

At any scale, the identity should feel like **commercial intelligence built inside the physical world of hospitality**.

## 9. Artistic Style Signature

The core artistic direction is **editorial hospitality realism combined with financial-document design and dark analytical interfaces**.

The visual genre draws from:

- premium editorial layouts
- documentary hospitality photography
- vintage financial print material
- contemporary dashboard design
- restrained industrial product photography

The strongest compositional technique is scale contrast.

A huge word or financial figure sits beside compact operational detail.

That creates tension between:

**what looks small operationally**  
and  
**what becomes large financially**

Lighting favors directional highlights and deep shadow. Stainless steel creates sharp reflections. Cream paper surfaces soften the harder industrial imagery.

Subjects are functional rather than decorative. A milk jug matters because it represents stock. A receipt matters because it represents cost. A phone matters because it delivers action.

Compositions should often leave one strong area of negative space for typography while allowing the opposite side to carry dense physical detail.

The cinematic language is intimate and observational. The viewer feels close to the counter, refrigerator, stockroom, or paperwork.

## 10. Cinematic Worldbuilding & Atmosphere

This brand exists inside a café after the decorative customer-facing layer has been removed.

The environment is the working side of hospitality.

It contains:

- stainless steel
- refrigeration
- storage shelves
- milk containers
- dark counters
- paperwork
- stock deliveries
- phones
- receipts
- monitoring tools

The atmosphere is warm but serious.

Light is directional. The space around the subject often falls into shadow. Reflections appear on metal surfaces. Paper catches warmer light.

The temperature feels slightly warm visually, but the physical environment still suggests refrigeration, steel, and back-of-house operations.

Movement should be subtle:

- milk pouring
- liquid dripping
- someone scrolling
- someone counting
- a receipt being checked
- inventory being moved
- a chart slowly rising

Sound, if the identity were translated into motion, would feel tactile and restrained: metal contact, paper movement, pouring liquid, refrigerator hum, keyboard taps, quiet café machinery.

Wardrobe should stay practical and dark. Aprons, black shirts, neutral workwear, and understated hospitality uniforms fit naturally.

Props should always serve the business story. Avoid decorative café clichés unless they support an operational point.

The world should feel **measured, observant, warm, disciplined, and financially aware**.

The defining sensory rule is simple: every physical detail should suggest that something can be counted, tracked, lost, protected, or recovered.
